Parents ask this more often than anything else at the clinic. It usually arrives
with a worry attached: if we take it out early, will the adult tooth come in
crooked?

The short answer: **the goal is to keep the tooth, not to remove it.**

## A primary tooth does two jobs no adult tooth can do for it

A primary tooth is not a placeholder waiting to fall out. While it is there, it
does two things:

- It **holds the space** so the adult tooth underneath can come up in the right
  place.
- It **teaches the jaw how to grow**, through the daily force of chewing.

Lose a primary tooth too early and the neighbouring teeth begin to tip into the
gap. By the time the adult tooth wants to come up, there is no longer a path.

That is the real mechanism behind crowded teeth — the lost space, not the
extraction itself.

## The only two moments to remove a primary tooth

**One: when it is already loose.** The root of a primary tooth dissolves
naturally as the adult tooth pushes up beneath it. A loose tooth means that
process is nearly finished.

**Two: when the adult tooth is already coming through** and the primary tooth has
not made way. The adult tooth is forced to deviate around it, and left long
enough it settles in the wrong position.

Outside those two moments, the right question is not "remove it or not" but
**"can it be kept, and how"**. A decayed primary tooth can usually be filled or
root-treated and held for the few years until it is due to go.

## Setting the record straight: extraction does not cause crooked adult teeth

Many parents believe it does, and that belief delays treatment.

**Removing a primary tooth for a good clinical reason does not make the adult
tooth come in crooked.**

Adult teeth come in crooked for other reasons:

- Not enough room on the arch — the arch is narrower than the teeth that must fit.
- A primary tooth lost too early and the space **lost with it**, because nothing
  held it.
- A long-standing habit steering how the jaw grows.
- A tooth bud that was sitting in the wrong place from the start.

The fear of extraction usually leads to a worse decision: leaving a decayed tooth
in place another year. The decay reaches the nerve, the child is woken by the
pain, and the tooth still comes out in the end — just later, after the child has
suffered for it.

## What to do instead

Bring the child in so we can tell you which group that tooth falls into: still
keepable, or due to go. That takes an examination and sometimes a radiograph. It
cannot be answered by looking from the outside.

If a tooth does have to come out ahead of schedule, there are ways to manage the
space it leaves — and that part matters more than the extraction itself.
